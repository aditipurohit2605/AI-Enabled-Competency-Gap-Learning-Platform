# Sampling Methods in Official Statistics

## Introduction
Sampling is the process of selecting a representative subset of individuals or units from within a statistical population to estimate characteristics of the whole population. In official statistical systems such as the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI) and National Sample Survey (NSS), rigorous sampling designs ensure that policy decisions are backed by reliable, cost-effective, and timely empirical data.

## Probability vs Non-Probability Sampling
In probability sampling, every unit in the target population has a known, non-zero probability of being selected. This allows statisticians to compute sampling errors and construct valid confidence intervals. Common probability sampling techniques include Simple Random Sampling, Systematic Sampling, Stratified Sampling, and Cluster Sampling. Conversely, non-probability sampling (such as convenience sampling, quota sampling, or purposive sampling) relies on subjective judgment and cannot yield statistically defensible margins of error.

## Simple Random Sampling (SRS)
Simple Random Sampling is the most fundamental probability sampling design. In SRS without replacement (SRSWOR), every subset of n units from a finite population of N units has an equal chance of selection: 1 / binom(N, n). While conceptually clean and unbiased, pure SRS is rarely practical for large-scale country-wide surveys because it requires an exhaustive, up-to-date sampling frame of every individual unit and often results in geographically scattered samples with high travel costs.

## Stratified Random Sampling
Stratified sampling divides a heterogeneous population into mutually exclusive, homogeneous subgroups termed strata (such as rural versus urban sectors, agro-climatic zones, or enterprise revenue brackets). Independent random samples are then drawn from each stratum. 

Stratification offers two critical advantages:
1. It reduces sampling variance because the variance within strata is significantly smaller than the variance across the entire population.
2. It guarantees adequate representation of small or critical administrative sub-populations. 

Allocation of sample sizes across strata can follow proportional allocation (sample size proportional to stratum size) or Neyman optimal allocation (larger samples allocated to larger and more variable strata).

## Cluster and Multi-Stage Sampling
In national household surveys, compiling a list of all households across the country is unfeasible. Instead, statisticians employ multi-stage sampling. 

In the first stage, Primary Sampling Units (PSUs)—typically Census Enumeration Blocks or villages—are selected with Probability Proportional to Size (PPS). In the second stage, households are listed within the selected PSUs, and Ultimate Sampling Units (USUs) are chosen via systematic or simple random sampling. 

While clustering increases the design effect (Deff) due to intra-cluster correlation, it dramatically reduces logistical costs and administrative overheads for field enumerators.

## Systematic Sampling
Systematic sampling selects units at fixed, regular intervals along an ordered sampling frame. If a population of size N is ordered and a sample of size n is required, the sampling interval k is computed as k = N / n. A random starting point r between 1 and k is chosen, followed by every k-th element (r, r+k, r+2k, ...). Systematic sampling is easy to execute in the field, provided the frame exhibits no hidden cyclical periodicity that coincides with the interval k.

## Sampling and Non-Sampling Errors
Survey estimates are subject to two distinct types of errors:
- Sampling Error: Arises due to observing a sample rather than the complete enumeration of the population. It decreases as the sample size increases and can be mathematically quantified.
- Non-Sampling Error: Errors arising at various stages of survey operations, such as coverage errors, frame deficiencies, respondent non-response, measurement and instrument biases, enumerator mistakes, and data processing errors. Unlike sampling errors, non-sampling errors can occur in both sample surveys and complete censuses, and do not necessarily decrease with larger sample sizes.
